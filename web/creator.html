Who makes it

I built Calibra to answer a simple question: how close can a model get to a La Liga result before kick-off? It is a personal project, not a product, and it publishes its misses as well as its hits.

Why football, why predictions

Football is the sport I watch most, and it is unusually hard to predict: one goal decides most matches, and the better team loses often enough to make you doubt the idea of a favourite at all.

I wanted to see what a model could honestly say about that. Not "who will win", but how likely each scoreline is — and then to let the season judge the answer, match by match.

What the site is trying to do

Every fixture gets a predicted scoreline before kick-off. When the result arrives, the prediction is scored and the tally stays on the site — including the matches it got badly wrong.

The point is not to look clever. It is to make a forecast inspectable: you can see the confidence, the whole scoreline grid, and the running record, and decide for yourself how much weight it deserves.

What building it taught me

A model that predicts football is not the same as a model that beats the bookmaker. The closing price already contains the team news, the lineups and the money, and it still scores better than anything I have built. I would rather say that plainly than hide it behind a headline.

The other lesson is about trust: a fixed, simple setup is easier to believe than one tuned until the numbers look good. So the method stays fixed, and the record reports what it actually did.

Find the code

The whole project — frontend, backend and the modelling pipeline — is open on GitHub. If you want the technical detail behind a forecast, How it works is the place to start.

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript on a small Node backend. No trackers, no ads, no accounts.
